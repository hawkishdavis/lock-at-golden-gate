Why levers, not a dam

They can only turn when the water levels are fairly equal on either side, because it is a matter of strength. It is the resistance of the water on either side that makes it strong. You are not having to hold water back and you are not having to push the water this way or that way. You are just channeling the flow a bit.

Hawk Davis, dictated 2026-09-18

A dam fights the ocean all day. A lever wall waits. At slack water there is nothing to fight, so the levers twist for the cost of turning them. Closed, the wall is a low dam only for the hours it needs to be one. The design ceiling is about 20 to 22 feet of head. Above that it does not hold everything back. It calms things down a heck of a lot, which is the point.

What one wall buys

Hydro energy

Every tide is a head difference. Throttled gaps are turbines waiting to be installed. Tidal power is the maintenance budget.

Pressure and cold

Deep, cold, moving seawater beside a city: hyperbaric wound care, cold compute, a Faraday pod for whoever needs to disappear from the spectrum for a week.

What the numbers are doing

Push on a closed wall is hydrostatic: seawater weighs about 64 pounds per cubic foot, so a head difference of h feet over a panel d feet deep loads the panel at roughly 64 × h × d pounds per foot of wall. The model uses a 40-foot panel on a sill. At 22 feet of head that is about 28 tons per foot, which is why the ocean-facing side is built heavier and why the wall is never asked to turn under load. The tide numbers are illustrative, not a hydrodynamic model of the Gate.

Most of the time it is a bathtub with low walls. Then a storm surge on a king tide comes, and the only question is whether the levers got to slack water in time. Two views. The why: the tide against the city, hour by hour, this year or twenty years out. The how: the barrier itself, and what each part of it is for.

Tide shape: the Gate's mixed semidiurnal tide, two highs and two lows a day of unequal height, a full cycle every 12 hours 25 minutes, slack water four times a day where the curve turns. Events are placed on the second day and sized from the record: the December 2024 king tide ran about 1.5 ft above mean higher high water; the January 1983 record about 2.9 ft; the January 3, 2026 tide plus wind surge about 3.8 ft. Years ahead adds the city's 2025 projection, mid: 1.0 ft by 2050, 2.2 by 2070, 4.9 by 2100; high: 1.2, 3.0, 6.6. Flood thresholds per spot are sourced in the README. The map is a sketch. Closed, the wall holds the Bay at the level it closed at, up to a 22 ft ceiling; permanent rise still comes in through the open gaps the rest of the year.

Why levers, not a dam

They can only turn when the water levels are fairly equal on either side, because it is a matter of strength. It is the resistance of the water on either side that makes it strong. You are not having to hold water back and you are not having to push the water this way or that way. You are just channeling the flow a bit.

Hawk Davis, dictated 2026-09-18

A dam fights the ocean all day. A lever wall waits. At slack water there is nothing to fight, so the levers twist for the cost of turning them. Closed, the wall is a low dam only for the hours it needs to be one. The trick of the whole thing is prediction: knowing the slack window before the surge, and closing in it. That is what the first view is for.

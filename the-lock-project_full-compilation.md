Other Dimensions & Proposals for Sea Level Rise 
Solutions 

Given the immense challenges of a barrier at the Golden Gate, current and proposed sea level 
rise adaptation strategies for the San Francisco Bay Area and other coastal cities focus on a 
multi-pronged approach, generally categorized as: 
- Protect: Using physical barriers to keep water out. 
- Accommodate: Adapting to living with water. 
- Retreat: Moving people and assets away from the water. 
Current Approaches in San Francisco Bay Area (and globally): 
1. Nature-Based Solutions (Soft Protection): These are increasingly prioritized due to 
their co-benefits (habitat restoration, recreation, water quality). 
○ Wetland Restoration: Restoring and expanding tidal marshes and mudflats. 
These natural buffers absorb wave energy, filter pollutants, and can accrete 
vertically with rising sea levels if sediment supply is sufficient. Projects like those 
in the South Bay (e.g., South Bay Salt Pond Restoration Project) are prime 
examples. 
○ Horizontal Levees/Berms: Broader, gentler sloped earthworks integrated with 
natural habitats, allowing for habitat migration and providing flood protection. 
○ Oyster Reefs and Seagrass Beds: These can dissipate wave energy, stabilize 
sediments, and provide critical habitat. 
2. Engineered or Hardened Solutions (Hard Protection): 
○ Seawalls and Levees: Traditional concrete or earthen barriers built along the 
shoreline. The Port of San Francisco, in collaboration with the U.S. Army Corps 
of Engineers, has released a Draft Plan for $13 billion in flood defenses along its 
7.5-mile jurisdiction, primarily involving seawalls and floodwalls. 
○ Tide Gates and Pump Stations: Installed in drainage systems to prevent tidal 
inundation of low-lying areas and pump out stormwater. 
○ Building Elevation/Retrofitting: Raising existing buildings or requiring new 
construction to be built at higher elevations. 
3. Accommodation Strategies: 
○ Elevated Infrastructure: Raising roads, utilities, and public spaces. Miami 
Beach has been notably active in raising roads and installing stormwater pumps. 
○ Floodable Public Spaces: Designing parks or plazas that can temporarily flood 
without significant damage, often incorporating water storage. Examples include 
Copenhagen's climate-resilient neighborhoods and Bangkok's Centenary Park. 
○ Updated Building Codes and Zoning: Implementing new regulations that guide 
development away from high-risk areas or mandate flood-resilient construction. 
4. Managed Retreat / Strategic Relocation: 
○ Voluntary Buyout Programs: Purchasing properties in highly vulnerable areas 
to allow nature to reclaim the land or to create buffer zones. 
○ Limiting Development in Floodplains: Restricting new construction in areas 
projected to be inundated by sea level rise. 
○ Relocation of Critical Infrastructure: Moving essential facilities (e.g., 
wastewater treatment plants) to higher ground. 

Key Principles Guiding Current Bay Area Efforts: 
- Regional Collaboration: Recognizing that "floodwaters don't stop at city borders," 
regional bodies like Save The Bay and the San Francisco Bay Conservation and 
Development Commission (BCDC) advocate for coordinated efforts across the nine Bay 
Area counties. 
- Nature-Based First: Prioritizing solutions that leverage natural processes and provide 
ecological co-benefits. 
- Equity and Vulnerable Communities: Focusing on protecting economically 
disadvantaged communities and socially vulnerable populations who are 
disproportionately affected by flooding. 
- Adaptive Management: Designing solutions that can be modified or expanded as sea 
level rise projections evolve.

Tsunami Risk in San Francisco 
While San Francisco is often more focused on seismic threats from faults like the San Andreas, 
the city is indeed vulnerable to tsunamis. The effects within the Bay are generally mitigated due 
to the narrow entrance (Golden Gate) and the broad, shallow nature of the Bay, which 
dissipates tsunami energy. However, outer coastlines and areas immediately inside the Golden 
Gate can still experience significant impacts. 
San Francisco could experience three types of tsunamis based on their origin: 
1. Distant Source Tsunamis: Generated by large earthquakes far across the Pacific 
Ocean (e.g., Alaska, Japan, Chile). 

○ Arrival Time: 4-15 hours, allowing for warning and evacuation. 
○ Likelihood: Moderate. 
○ Examples: The 2011 Tohoku tsunami (Japan) caused noticeable surges and 
currents in San Francisco Bay, leading to minor damage to docks and boats, 
though no widespread inundation. The 1964 Alaska earthquake also generated a 
tsunami that impacted the California coast, including the Bay. 
2. Regional Source Tsunamis: Generated by earthquakes closer to California, but still 
hundreds of miles away (e.g., Cascadia Subduction Zone off Oregon/Washington). 
○ Arrival Time: 1-1.5 hours. 
○ Likelihood: Moderate. 
3. Near Source Tsunamis: Generated by local offshore earthquakes or underwater 
landslides directly off the California coast. 
○ Arrival Time: 10-15 minutes, offering very little warning. 
○ Likelihood: Low, but potentially the most dangerous due to rapid arrival. 
○ Examples: The 1906 San Francisco earthquake generated a small tsunami 
recorded at a tide gauge at the opening of San Francisco Bay, which was likely 
caused by underwater landslides. 
Notable Tsunami Events (Directly or Indirectly Affecting San Francisco): 
- 1906 San Francisco Earthquake Tsunami: While the earthquake itself was the primary 
disaster, a small, localized tsunami was generated, likely by underwater landslides. It 
was observed at the Presidio tide gauge. (Source: USGS - Tsunami Record from the 
Great 1906 San Francisco Earthquake). 
- 1964 Alaska Earthquake Tsunami: This massive M9.2 earthquake generated a 
Pacific-wide tsunami. While Crescent City in Northern California was devastated, San 
Francisco experienced noticeable but less destructive surges within the Bay. 
- 2011 Tohoku Earthquake (Japan) Tsunami: This M9.1 earthquake in Japan generated 
a trans-Pacific tsunami that reached the California coast. In San Francisco Bay, docks 
were damaged, and currents were strong, but widespread inundation did not occur. 
There were significant impacts and fatalities in other parts of the California coast, 
highlighting the varying vulnerability. 
Tsunami Preparedness in San Francisco: 
San Francisco has a robust emergency management system, including tsunami warning 
protocols through the National Weather Service and the California Office of Emergency 
Services. Evacuation zones are identified, particularly for coastal areas and low-lying regions 
within the Bay. Public education emphasizes moving to higher ground immediately if a natural 
warning sign (e.g., strong earthquake, sudden recession of water) occurs, even before official 
alerts. 
The potential impacts of a large tsunami on San Francisco would primarily be felt along the 
outer coast, within the Golden Gate Strait, and along immediate waterfronts. Strong currents, 
surging water, and debris could damage piers, marinas, and low-lying infrastructure. The city's 

geography, with its hills and the constricted Bay entrance, generally offers some natural 
protection against the most devastating inland inundation compared to open coastal plains.

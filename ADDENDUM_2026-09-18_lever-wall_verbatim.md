# The lever wall, a baby step

Dictated by the author, 2026-09-18, in the Chief of Documentation session. Verbatim, transcription noise only cleaned. This is the addendum to The Lock at Golden Gate Bridge.

"There's a little bit of misconception. It's like, how the heck would it look and what would it do? It's basically, not much, for most of the time. It's literally just a bathtub with low walls most of the time. Not even really noticeable most of the time, kind of.

And here's the interesting engineering piece of it. Now this is where I kind of get a little crazy, right? Because there's water on both sides of the lock. It doesn't have to be a still bath, a big, like a dam. I think of it a little bit more like levers, like window levers, the twist. You either have them open with the flow, or twist and closed. You could say, sure, that's kind of leaky, and eventually maybe it would have to be sealed closed all the time if necessary, if it got that high. But right now we're really more concerned with storm surge and high tide, and it is worth it, because the infrastructure can also then just be locked in and welded at a future time. But for right now, just levers. Levers turning to throttle or obstruct water flow.

And don't be mad. This is kind of a new idea, but not really. It's just a baby step. So you start with just one wall of levers. And they are really just going to wait till the low tide to then shut, for example. Or they wait till the high tide to shut. It doesn't really matter. They can shut whenever. Probably they can only turn, open, move either way, when the water levels are fairly equal on either side, because it's a matter of strength, right? Because it's the resistance of the water on either side that makes it strong. You're not having to hold water back and you're not having to push the water this way or that way. You're just channeling the flow a bit.

The outside of the bay, there will be more pressure coming into the bay, so it would need to be designed slightly more robust for that potential, which is the point. But really, up to maybe only 20 feet, 22 feet, let's say, something like that. Anything over that, it would just calm things down a heck of a lot.

I could just publish what I have currently and then just add this to it as a side branch, but I like it when it's cohesive, you know. Baby steps. This gets all the good things: you've got the hydro energy, you've got the pressure, hyperbaric wound care, you've got the cold compute, you've got potentially the billionaire Faraday pod, and possibly the tourism. Stay in one of the, stay in a channel, in the channel, right in the middle of the channel, be one of the levers. Maybe you could rent out a lever, and your whole lever, you know, the cool experience: it turns, and you turn with it."

Editor's numbers for scale, added the same night, public sources, approximate: the Golden Gate Bridge deck is about 90 feet wide; the main span is 4,200 feet and the whole bridge about 8,980 feet; the channel under it is roughly 300 to 380 feet deep at its deepest; each tide moves on the order of a quarter of the Bay's water through the Gate, about 1.5 billion cubic meters, which is why exchange matters as much as protection.
